Travelling with a Disability in WA: Planning Trips That Actually Work

Western Australia is made for road trips and slow weekends — turquoise coastline, forests, wineries, quokkas. Travelling with a disability takes more planning, but people with high support needs, including spinal cord injuries, travel throughout WA all the time. The difference between a stressful trip and a great one is almost always preparation.

Choosing where to go

Plenty of WA destinations have invested in access. Rottnest Island offers accessible accommodation options and equipment hire. Many South West towns, wineries and caves regions have accessible cabins, boardwalk trails and adapted experiences. Closer to home, day trips through the Swan Valley or along the coast keep things simple while you build confidence.

Verify, do not assume

The single most important travel habit: call ahead and ask specific questions. Accessible means different things to different operators. Ask for door widths, step-free paths, bathroom photos, bed heights and hoist availability if you need it. Photos tell the truth better than checkboxes on a website.

Accommodation checklist

  • Step-free entry and genuinely level access throughout — including to the parking
  • Roll-in shower, shower chair or commode availability, and rail placement
  • Bed height compatible with your transfers, and space beside the bed for equipment
  • Distance to dining, pool and other facilities
  • Nearest hospital or after-hours medical care, for peace of mind

Pack for your body, not just the destination

Build a packing list you reuse every trip: continence supplies with generous spares, medications plus extras, pressure cushion, skin care items, chargers for powered equipment, and a repair kit for your chair. If you have a spinal cord injury, plan around temperature regulation — WA heat is serious, and long car journeys need scheduled pressure relief and hydration stops.

Getting around

Wheelchair-accessible vehicle hire exists in Perth but books out, so reserve early. Regional taxis with accessible vehicles can be scarce — check availability at your destination before you commit. For longer journeys, plan fuel and rest stops around accessible toilets; the National Public Toilet Map is a genuinely useful planning tool.

Consider travelling with support

Taking a support worker on a trip — for transfers, personal care, driving and general logistics — can turn an ambitious itinerary into a relaxed one. NDIS funding may cover support worker time while travelling in some circumstances; talk to your plan manager or support coordinator about what applies to your plan.

Start with a weekend

If you have not travelled since your injury or diagnosis, a single night somewhere close to Perth is the perfect trial run. You will learn more from one overnight stay than from months of research — and most people come home already planning the next one.

Flying and longer trips

If your WA travels extend to flights — Broome, Exmouth, the Kimberley — the same planning principles apply with extra lead time. Contact the airline well in advance about mobility equipment, battery specifications for powered wheelchairs, and assistance through the airport. Book aisle seats and early boarding, plan pressure relief for the flight, and carry essential medications and continence supplies in your cabin luggage, never checked. Travel insurance that properly covers your equipment and pre-existing conditions is worth researching carefully; a damaged wheelchair far from home is a very different problem than a damaged suitcase.

Learn from every trip

Keep simple notes after each trip: what worked, what you over-packed, which accommodation genuinely delivered on its access promises, which operators went the extra mile. Over a few trips this becomes a personal travel playbook that makes each journey easier than the last — and a goldmine for other travellers with disability, if you are inclined to share reviews. WA's accessible tourism scene improves partly because travellers speak up: praise the operators who do it well, and give honest feedback to those who could do better.
