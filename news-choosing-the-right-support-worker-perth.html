Choosing the Right Support Worker in Perth: What Really Matters

Inviting a support worker into your home is a big decision. This is someone who will share your mornings, learn your routines, and stand beside you through the ordinary and the important moments of your life. Finding the right person is about far more than qualifications on paper.

Start with your life, not their resume

Before you interview anyone, get clear on what your days actually look like. What does a good morning feel like for you? Which tasks need clinical skill, and which simply need a capable, respectful pair of hands? When you understand your own rhythm, you can quickly tell whether someone will fit into it or fight against it.

Think about personality as well as skills

Skills can be taught. Warmth, patience and reliability are harder to train. Many people living with disability in Perth tell us the best support workers are the ones who feel like a natural presence in the house — someone you can be quiet with, laugh with, and be completely yourself around.

Questions worth asking

  • Experience with your specific needs: if you live with a spinal cord injury, ask directly about their experience with transfers, pressure care, bowel and bladder routines, and recognising complications.
  • Training and supervision: who trains them, and who checks their work? Quality providers invest in ongoing clinical oversight, not just an induction day.
  • Consistency: will you see the same faces each week, or a rotating cast of strangers? Consistency builds trust and safety.
  • Communication: how do they handle changes, cancellations and feedback? You should never feel like a burden for speaking up.

Red flags to watch for

Be cautious of providers who cannot tell you who will be turning up, who talk about you rather than to you, or who seem more interested in filling hours than understanding your goals. Support that is rushed, impersonal or inflexible rarely improves with time.

You are allowed to choose — and to change your mind

Under the NDIS you have choice and control over who supports you. If something does not feel right, you can say so. A good provider will welcome the conversation and adjust; a great one will have asked you first.

How Dora approaches it

Trial shifts and settling in

However careful the matching process, the real test is time together. Ask whether a provider offers introductory or trial shifts before you commit to a regular roster. Use the first few weeks deliberately: notice whether the worker arrives on time, follows your routine rather than their habits, and asks before changing anything. Give feedback early and often — small corrections in week one prevent entrenched frustrations by month three. A provider who checks in with you after the first shifts, rather than assuming silence means satisfaction, is showing you how they will behave for the whole relationship.

Building a team, not relying on one person

Even a perfect support worker gets sick, takes leave, and eventually moves on. Sustainable support usually means a small, consistent team of two or three people who all know your routines well, so cover never means starting from scratch with a stranger. Ask how a provider handles handovers between workers, and whether your preferences and routines are documented properly so knowledge lives with the team, not in one person's head. For people with spinal cord injuries, where routines are precise and the consequences of errors are real, this depth of backup is not a luxury — it is the difference between support you can build a life on and support you have to supervise.
