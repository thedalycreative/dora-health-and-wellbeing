Dignity in Personal Care: What It Looks Like and Why It Matters

Personal care — showering, dressing, toileting, continence support — sits at the most intimate edge of disability support. Done with genuine respect, it becomes an unremarkable part of the day, like brushing your teeth. Done carelessly, it can quietly erode confidence and self-worth. Dignity is not an abstract value; it is a set of concrete behaviours you are entitled to expect.

Dignity is built from small things

Asking, not assuming

A dignified support worker asks how you like things done — water temperature, order of tasks, which products, how much help you want today — and keeps asking as things change. Your routine belongs to you, not to a care plan template.

Privacy as a default

Doors closed. Bodies covered whenever possible. Conversations about personal care held privately, never across a room or in front of visitors. These habits should be automatic, not something you have to request.

Talking with you, not over you

You are a person having a shower, not a task being completed. Good support workers follow your lead — some people like chat and humour, others prefer calm quiet. Both are dignity when they are your choice.

Unhurried time

Rushed care is undignified care. Providers who book realistic visit lengths, rather than squeezing personal care into impossible windows, are telling you something important about their values.

Dignity and skill go together

For people living with spinal cord injury, personal care also involves real clinical competence — safe transfers, careful skin checks, correct continence routines. Nervous or undertrained workers often compensate by taking over completely, which strips away autonomy. Confidence and competence are what allow a support worker to relax, slow down and let you direct your own care.

What you can do if care does not feel right

  • Say something early. Most issues are habits, not intentions, and a good worker will genuinely want the feedback.
  • Put preferences in writing. Ask your provider to record your routine and preferences so new workers arrive prepared.
  • Escalate if nothing changes. You can raise concerns with the provider, and formally with the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission if needed. You never have to accept care that diminishes you.

The Dora standard

Consistency is a dignity issue

It is difficult to overstate how much continuity matters in personal care. Explaining your routine, preferences and body to a new stranger every few weeks is tiring at best and demeaning at worst. A small, stable team who know your care intimately means you never have to narrate your own shower again. When new workers do join — as they sometimes must — a dignified provider ensures they arrive already briefed, shadow an experienced colleague first, and are introduced to you properly rather than simply appearing at the door.

Culture, language and personal identity

Dignity also means care that fits who you are. That might mean support workers of a particular gender for intimate care, respect for cultural or religious practices around modesty and bathing, or simply people who share your language or sense of humour. These are legitimate preferences, not demanding requests, and any quality Perth provider should accommodate them as a matter of course. The NDIS principle of choice and control extends fully into the bathroom — arguably it matters most there, in the moments when you are most vulnerable and most dependent on the character of the person assisting you.
